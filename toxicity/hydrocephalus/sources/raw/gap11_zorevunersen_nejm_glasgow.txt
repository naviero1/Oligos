Abstract 
 
Background 
Dravet syndrome is a severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy primarily caused by 
SCN1A haploinsufficiency. Here, we describe the effects of zorevunersen, an antisense 
oligonucleotide designed to upregulate NaV1.1 sodium channels, in patients with Dravet syndrome.    
Methods 
Patients with Dravet syndrome aged 2–18 years on standard antiseizure medications were enrolled 
in two Phase 1/2a, open-label, multi-center studies (MONARCH/ADMIRAL). Patients received single 
(SAD) or multiple (MAD) ascending doses of zorevunersen ranging from 10–70 mg (SAD) or 20–70 
mg (MAD). Eligible patients rolled over to the SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING open-label extension 
(OLE) studies, where they continued receiving zorevunersen (≤45 mg) every 4 months. Safety, 
pharmacokinetics, and clinical effects of zorevunersen were evaluated. 
Results 
81 patients were enrolled across MONARCH/ADMIRAL. As of May 30, 2025, 75 patients had entered 
the OLE studies. Most TEAEs were mild or moderate. The most common TEAE in 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL and SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING was post lumbar puncture syndrome 
(24.7%) and CSF protein increase (45.3%), respectively. One participant had suspected unexpected 
serious adverse reactions, one discontinued the study, two died due to SUDEP, and one died of 
malnutrition. Patients who received 70 mg zorevunersen (SAD/MAD) in MONARCH/ADMIRAL 
followed by ≤45 mg in SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING experienced median seizure reductions of 
58.82%–90.91% over the first 20 months of the OLEs. Improvements were seen in overall clinical 


===PAGE 5===
status, quality of life, and adaptive behavior following continued dosing for up to 36 months in the 
OLEs. 
Conclusions 
These results support the continued development of zorevunersen as a potential disease-
modifying treatment for Dravet syndrome. (Funding: Stoke Therapeutics; Trial registration, 
MONARCH:NCT04442295; ADMIRAL:ISRCTN99651026; SWALLOWTAIL:NCT04740476; 
LONGWING:ISRCTN12811235)

Introduction 
Dravet syndrome is a severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy caused primarily 
by variants in one copy of the voltage-gated sodium channel type 1 alpha subunit (SCN1A) gene, 
resulting in SCN1A haploinsufficiency and reduced NaV1.1 sodium channel expression. 1,2 These 
channels are highly expressed in GABAergic inhibitory interneurons and some glutamatergic 
neurons in the brain. 3,4 Reductions in NaV1.1 disrupt the excitatory-inhibitory balance, leading to 
general hyperexcitability and seizures. 3,5  
The clinical phenotype of Dravet syndrome includes a spectrum of symptoms that emerge 
early and evolve. 6 Most patients experience cognitive deficits, communication and behavioral 
impairments, motor dysfunction, growth delays, and autistic traits. 7-13 Difficulties with feeding, poor 
appetite, and weight loss are also common. 8,14 These impairments reduce quality of life (QoL) for 
patients and caregivers. 15 Additionally, the risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is 
higher in patients with Dravet syndrome compared with the general population with epilepsy. 16,17 
The current standard of care (SOC) for Dravet syndrome includes antiseizure medications 
(ASMs), dietary therapy, and/or neuromodulation with the goal of optimized seizure control. 18 
However, these treatments fail to control seizures in most patients, and their effect on non-seizure 
symptoms is minimal. 9,19 Moreover, reductions in seizure frequency may not necessarily 
correspond to improvements in non-seizure outcomes, which are a major concern of caregivers. 8,19 
Consequently, disease-modifying therapies directly addressing the underlying channelopathy to 
improve both seizure and non-seizure symptoms are needed. 11,15,20 
Zorevunersen (STK-001), an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO), is designed to address the 
channelopathy underlying Dravet syndrome. In eukaryotes, precursor messenger ribonucleic acid 
(mRNA) matures into mRNA via splicing, the process by which introns are removed and exons 
joined together. 21,22 Non-productive (poison) exons contain a premature termination codon, which, 


===PAGE 7===
if included in mRNA, results in its degradation. 21,22 Zorevunersen binds to wild-type SCN1A pre-
mRNA to prevent non‑productive exon inclusion, thereby increasing productive mRNA synthesis 
and NaV1.1 protein expression. 21,23 Restoring NaV1.1 to physiological levels in the brain could 
improve overall function, including cognition and behavior, in patients with Dravet syndrome. Here, 
we present results from the Phase 1/2a MONARCH and ADMIRAL studies, which investigated the 
safety, pharmacokinetics, and effects on seizures, QoL, and adaptive behavior of zorevunersen in 
patients with Dravet syndrome, and interim results (May 30, 2025 data cut) from the ongoing 
SWALLOWTAIL and LONGWING open-label extensions (OLE) studies. 
 


===PAGE 8===
Methods 
 
Study design 
 
MONARCH and ADMIRAL were open-label, multi-center studies conducted in the USA and 
UK, respectively, to facilitate broader recruitment/access. MONARCH/ADMIRAL patients were 
followed for ≥6 months after receiving the last dose of zorevunersen. Those who completed the 
studies and met enrollment criteria continued receiving zorevunersen in SWALLOWTAIL and 
LONGWING. Figure S1 depicts the study timeline.  
MONARCH included a single ascending dose (SAD) part, in which patients received a single 
dose of 10, 20, 30, 45, or 70 mg zorevunersen on Day 1, and a multiple ascending dose (MAD) part, 
in which patients received repeating doses at 20, 30, or 45 mg, administered on Days 1, 29, and 57, 
to evaluate dose level and dosing interval (see the Supplementary Appendix for sentinel dosing). 
ADMIRAL had MAD cohorts with dose levels of 30, 45, and 70 mg, administered on Days 1, 57, and 
85. To evaluate a reduced dosing schedule at the highest dose level, six patients in the MAD 70 mg 
cohort received only two doses of zorevunersen, on Days 1 and 57. 
The OLE dosing regimen consists of three initial doses, administered on Day 1, Month 4 
(Week 16), and Month 8 (Week 32). Patients who complete these initial three doses receive 
additional doses every 4 months (16 weeks) until study discontinuation. All OLE patients are now 
receiving continued doses of 45 mg zorevunersen. Details on intrathecal zorevunersen 
administration, dosing history, and End-of-Study/Follow-up visits are provided in the 
Supplementary Appendix. 
 
Study oversight 


===PAGE 9===
 
Review boards or ethics committees at each participating institution approved the study 
protocols. Studies were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set by the Declaration 
of Helsinki and the International Council on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All 
patients or their legal representatives provided written informed consent.  
The sponsor (Stoke Therapeutics) provided the study drug, developed the study protocols, 
and participated in data collection. All authors contributed to data collection, analysis, and 
interpretation, and affirm adherence to study protocols and data accuracy/integrity. The sponsor 
made the decision to publish the manuscript in agreement with all authors, who reviewed and 
approved the final version. Confidentiality agreements between the authors and sponsor were 
signed. 
 
Patients 
 
MONARCH enrolled patients aged 2 to 18 years (inclusive); ADMIRAL enrolled patients aged 
≥2 to <18 years. Otherwise, eligibility criteria were comparable. Eligible patients had to experience 
≥4 convulsive seizures during the initial 28 days of the Observation Period despite at least 1 
concomitant ASM and prior use of ≥2 ASMs. All MONARCH/ADMIRAL patients had an established 
diagnosis of Dravet syndrome confirmed by an external consortium with expertise in the condition, 
together with a pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or variant of uncertain significance in SCN1A.  
To enter SWALLOWTAIL or LONGWING, patients must have completed MONARCH or 
ADMIRAL within the prior 4 weeks (unless approved by the sponsor) with an acceptable safety 
profile per investigator judgement. Study protocols include detailed inclusion and exclusion 
criteria. 


===PAGE 10===
 
Endpoints 
 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL primary endpoints included the safety profile, plasma 
pharmacokinetics (PK), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) exposure of single and/or multiple doses of 
zorevunersen. For SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING, the primary endpoint was the safety profile of 
multiple doses of zorevunersen. 
Across studies, common secondary endpoints included change from baseline in convulsive 
seizure frequency calculated over 1-month (28-day) intervals, change in overall clinical status as 
measured by the Clinical and Caregiver Global Impression of Change (CGI-C and CaGI-C, 
respectively) assessments, and change from baseline in patient QoL as measured by the EuroQol 
visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) component of the EuroQoL 5-dimension (Youth) (EQ-5D-Y) 
instrument. Changes in the CGI-C and CaGI-C were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 
for “very much improved” to 7 for “very much worse”. 24 The EQ-VAS is a vertical scale from 0 to 100, 
with 0 representing the worst imaginable health to 100 representing the best imaginable health. 25 
Secondary endpoints specific to SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING included plasma pharmacokinetics 
and CSF exposure to multiple doses of zorevunersen. 
Exploratory endpoints included additional measures of seizure frequency and change from 
baseline in adaptive behavior as measured by the Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales, Third Edition 
(Vineland-3) in ADMIRAL and SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING (see the Supplementary Appendix for 
details on Vineland-3 domains and subdomains). Vineland-3 raw scores range from 0 to 116, with 
the upper limit varying by subdomain. 26 Caregivers have been reported to consider improvements 
of 1–3 raw score points as meaningful. 27 
 


===PAGE 11===
Statistical analysis 
 
No formal sample size calculations or hypothesis testing were performed for 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL as they were primarily dose-finding studies. See the Supplementary Appendix 
for definitions of the safety, pharmacokinetic and clinical evaluable sets.  
Seizure frequencies were calculated per 1-month (28-day) intervals throughout the studies; 
intervals were aligned to begin relative to the most recent dose of zorevunersen. Baseline seizure 
frequencies were calculated from the month (28 days) prior to the first dose in 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL. For the primary analysis, seizure data intervals were excluded if seizure data 
were available for <50% of the days within a 1-month (28-day) interval, or, in MONARCH/ADMIRAL 
only, for timepoints following ASM modification (Table S1 and results in Supplementary Appendix). 
Sensitivity analyses of convulsive seizure frequency were performed including all collected seizure 
data with no exclusion for ASM modification, with the results supporting study conclusions. All 
seizure analyses are descriptive, with medians and 95% confidence intervals reported. The widths 
of the confidence intervals were not adjusted for multiplicity and should not be used to infer 
treatment effects. 
Mixed-effects models for repeated measures (MMRM) were used to estimate outcome 
trajectories over time for the CaGI-C, CGI-C, EuroQol visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS), and 
Vineland-3. This approach enhances interpretability, maintains consistency across time points, 
and adjusts for potential confounding factors inherent in longitudinal data analyses. See the 
statistical analysis plans for additional information on MMRMs. 
Missing data were not imputed; MMRMs assume that data are missing at random and 
include all available data. All statistical analyses were performed using Windows SAS® Version 9.4 
and JMP Pro 18.2 or higher.

Results 
Study population 
 
MONARCH was conducted from June 29, 2020, to November 16, 2023; ADMIRAL was 
conducted from July 29, 2021, to November 07, 2023. Of the 81 patients on a background of SOC 
ASMs who received ≥1 dose of zorevunersen (MONARCH, n=62; ADMIRAL, n=19; Figures S2 and 
S3), 80 completed the studies (MONARCH, n=61; ADMIRAL, n=19). SWALLOWTAIL began on 
January 20, 2021; LONGWING began on May 9, 2022. As of May 30, 2025, 58/61 (95.1%) MONARCH 
completers rolled over into SWALLOWTAIL, and 17/19 (89.5%) ADMIRAL completers rolled over into 
either LONGWING or SWALLOWTAIL (Figures S4 and S5). Of these 75 patients, 45/59 (76.3%) in 
SWALLOWTAIL and 13/16 (81.3%) in LONGWING remain enrolled.  
Baseline patient characteristics between MONARCH/ADMIRAL and between 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING were comparable (Table 1). Mean (standard deviation) patient ages at 
baseline in MONARCH/ADMIRAL and SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING were 9.9 (5.05) and 10.4 (5.04) 
years, respectively. In MONARCH/ADMIRAL, 81.5% of patients were taking ≥3 ASMs at baseline; the 
most common were clobazam (70.4%), fenfluramine (49.4%), cannabidiol (44.4%), and valproate 
compounds (44.4%). Patient demographics were representative of the expected gender distribution 
and prevalence of Dravet syndrome in the respective study regions (Table S2).  
 
Safety 
 
78/81 patients (96.3%) in MONARCH/ADMIRAL and 75/75 patients (100.0%) in 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING experienced ≥1 treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) (Table 2); 


===PAGE 13===
most TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity. The most common TEAE in MONARCH/ADMIRAL 
was post lumbar puncture syndrome (24.7%), while the most common TEAE in 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING was CSF protein increase (45.3%) (Tables S3 and S4). Serious TEAEs 
were reported in 18 patients (22.2%) in MONARCH/ADMIRAL and in 22 patients (29.3%) in 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING (see the Supplementary Appendix) and were considered treatment-
related in one patient. The most common serious TEAEs across both Phase 1/2a and OLE studies 
were seizure (MONARCH/ADMIRAL: 4.9%; SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING: 5.3%) and status 
epilepticus (MONARCH/ADMIRAL: 1.2%; SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING: 5.3%; Tables S5 and S6).  
Treatment-related TEAEs were reported in 24/81 (29.6%) and 40/75 (53.3%) patients in 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL and SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING, respectively. Treatment-related TEAEs were 
mild or moderate in severity, except for events in one patient in the 70 mg MAD cohort of ADMIRAL 
who had suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions. This patient experienced procedural 
vomiting and reduced fluid intake that led to hospitalization 1 day after their second dose of 
zorevunersen and resolved after approximately 1 week. CSF protein elevations were not observed. 
Approximately 2 months after receiving a third dose of zorevunersen, this patient developed 
cortical visual impairment, eye movement disorder, and neurological decompensation. These 
events were present at the end of the study and are considered treatment-related due to lack of an 
alternative explanation. The most common treatment-related TEAE across both Phase 1/2a and 
OLE studies was CSF protein increase (MONARCH/ADMIRAL: 13.6%; SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING: 
44.0%; Supplementary Appendix, Tables S7–S10). No patients experienced TEAEs of 
hydrocephalus or increased intracranial pressure; one patient experienced a TEAE of CSF 
pleocytosis with a CSF white blood cell count of 11μL. The Supplementary Appendix and Tables 
S11 and S12 summarize TEAEs related to CSF collection or study drug administration. 


===PAGE 14===
 
One SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING patient experienced a TEAE that led to study drug 
discontinuation/study withdrawal (see the Supplementary Appendix).  
Two patients (one in MONARCH and one in SWALLOWTAIL) died due to SUDEP, consistent 
with their underlying disease, and one SWALLOWTAIL patient died of malnutrition (see the 
Supplementary Appendix). 
 
Pharmacokinetics 
 
In MONARCH/ADMIRAL, plasma zorevunersen levels generally increased proportionally 
with dose (Figure S6). Zorevunersen was detected in CSF up to 6 months after the last dose in all 
cohorts. In the MAD cohorts, zorevunersen levels in CSF increased 3 months after the last dose, 
suggesting zorevunersen accumulation in CSF following monthly/bimonthly dosing (Figure S7). 
In SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING, following zorevunersen administration every 4 months, no 
apparent plasma accumulation was observed across all dose levels. Conversely, mean trough CSF 
concentrations generally increased with subsequent dosing over the first year before reaching close 
to steady state levels.  
See the Supplementary Appendix, Figures S6 and S7, and Table S13 for additional PK data.  
 
Seizure frequency 
 
Seizure data were available for most patients at all timepoints (Table S1 and results in 
Supplementary Appendix). The median (range) Phase 1/2a baseline convulsive seizure frequency 
per 1-month (28 days) was 17.0 (4.0–2,335.4) across patients (n=77) and ranged from 8.3–64.0 
across dose cohorts (Tables 1 and S14). Figures 1A and 1B illustrate the median change from 


===PAGE 15===
baseline in convulsive seizure frequencies over 1-month (28-day) intervals across different 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL dose cohorts. Median reductions in convulsive seizure frequency at 3 and 6 
months after the last dose were 42.81% and 57.30%, respectively, in patients who received a single 
dose of 70 mg zorevunersen, and 84.80% and 73.61%, respectively, in patients who received 2 or 3 
doses of 70 mg zorevunersen (Supplementary Appendix, Figures S8 and S9). The proportions of 
patients experiencing ≥50%, ≥75%, and 100% reduction in convulsive seizure frequency, convulsive 
seizure-free days, and total seizure frequencies are described in the Supplementary Appendix and 
Tables S15–S17. 
Percent change in convulsive seizure frequency from Phase 1/2a baseline among 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING patients are presented in Figures 1C and 1D. In patients who received 
doses of <70 mg zorevunersen in Phase 1/2a, median reductions from Phase 1/2a baseline ranged 
from 6.94%–77.38% over 1-month (28-day) intervals through Month 36 of the OLEs (Figure 1C). 
Median reductions in patients who received single or multiple doses of 70 mg zorevunersen in 
MONARCH/ADMIRAL ranged from 58.82%–90.91% over 1-month (28-day) intervals through Month 
20 of the OLEs (Figure 1D). 
 
Overall clinical status and QoL 
 
In MONARCH/ADMIRAL, clinicians and caregivers rated 84.2% and 94.8% of patients who 
received single or multiple doses of 70 mg zorevunersen, respectively, as “minimally improved” to 
“very much improved” at 6 months after last dose (Figures 2A and 2B).  
In SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING, clinicians and caregivers rated 94.7% of all enrolled patients 
as “minimally improved” to “very much improved” at Month 36 compared with OLE baseline (Figure 
2C; Supplementary Appendix, Figures S10 and S11).  


===PAGE 16===
 
 Exploratory cross-study comparisons adjusted for baseline prognostic covariates suggest 
that improvements in overall clinical status among patients with Dravet syndrome treated with 
zorevunersen appear to differ from the minimal change detected among patients from the 
BUTTERFLY natural history study (Figure S12). The QoL data are consistent with improved 
outcomes (Supplementary Appendix, Figures S13 and S14). 
 
Adaptive behavior 
 
Figures 3A and 3B display changes in Vineland-3 subdomains among ADMIRAL patients 
who rolled over into LONGWING and among OLE patients, respectively.  
Improvements in Expressive and Receptive Communication subdomains, measured as 
least squares mean change in raw scores from ADMIRAL baseline, were 3.29 and 5.21, respectively, 
at the end of ADMIRAL (Month 9) and 6.55 and 10.00, respectively, by Month 17 of LONGWING 
(Figure 3A). At Month 36 of the OLEs, the least squares mean change in raw scores from OLE 
baseline in Expressive and Receptive Communication were 7.56 and 6.06, respectively (Figure 3B).  
Exploratory cross-study comparisons adjusted for baseline prognostic covariates suggest 
that changes in adaptive behavior among patients with Dravet syndrome treated with zorevunersen 
appear to differ with respect to the minimal improvements in BUTTERFLY patients; BUTTERFLY is a 
prospective study designed to capture natural history of the disease (Figure S15).28 
 
Real-life impacts on activities of daily living associated with the clinical data described here 
are conveyed by the clinical improvements recorded in an ADMIRAL/LONGWING patient (see the 
Supplementary Appendix for details). See Supplementary video of this patient before/after 
zorevunersen treatment.

Discussion 
Treatment of patients with zorevunersen did not result in any apparent safety signal. The 
results reported here support the continued development of zorevunersen as a potential disease-
modifying treatment for Dravet syndrome. Most treatment-related TEAEs were mild or moderate, 
with the most common across MONARCH/ADMIRAL and SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING being 
elevated CSF protein as observed on routine laboratory screening. Similar CSF protein elevations 
have been reported in other FDA-approved intrathecal ASOs. 29-31 Increased CSF protein following 
repeated nusinersen dosing has been reported and may be a possible therapeutic marker. 32 No 
patients experienced TEAEs of hydrocephalus or increased intracranial pressure. One patient 
experienced CSF pleocytosis. Ongoing safety monitoring includes neurological examinations and 
CSF laboratory testing.  
 Zorevunersen treatment was associated with improvements in seizure outcomes, overall 
clinical status, QoL, and adaptive behavior in patients with Dravet syndrome refractory to SOC 
treatment with ASMs. While the study design did not support formal statistical comparisons among 
dose groups, median seizure reductions were largest in patients receiving multiple initial doses of 
70 mg, consistent with a dose-effect relationship. Continued treatment in 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING appeared to show an association with stabilized reductions in 
convulsive seizure frequency over 36 months. Overall, the seizure data suggest differences with 
respect to the natural history study, 28 in which patients with Dravet syndrome on best-available 
SOC treatment on average showed no improvement in convulsive seizure frequency through 24 
months.  
Consistent with zorevunersen’s mechanism of upregulating SCN1A in the context of the 
most common underlying cause of Dravet syndrome, treatment with zorevunersen appeared to be 
associated with improvements in overall clinical status, QoL, and adaptive behavior. No approved 


===PAGE 18===
therapies address the underlying cause of Dravet syndrome, and caregivers have indicated that 
alleviating non-seizure aspects are important for new treatments. 33 Continuing improvements were 
noted across Vineland-3 subdomains. These include Expressive and Receptive Communication, 
which caregivers view as one of the most important disease aspects to change following treatment. 
27,33 The changes in adaptive behavior and overall clinical status among patients with Dravet 
syndrome treated with zorevunersen appeared to be different from those observed in the natural 
history study and warrant further evaluation as part of ongoing clinical development.   
Patient demographics in MONARCH/ADMIRAL and SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING reflected 
the expected gender distribution and prevalence of Dravet syndrome in the respective study regions 
(Table S2). Study limitations include the open-label design, lack of placebo or sham control, small 
sample sizes, lack of formal power calculations, uncorrected multiple comparisons, and variability 
in baseline measures (e.g., seizure frequency across dose groups), all of which prevent us from 
drawing conclusions about efficacy. Moreover, Vineland-3 and QoL measures may be susceptible 
to caregiver bias due to the open-label design. As MONARCH/ADMIRAL and 
SWALLOWTAIL/LONGWING were primarily safety and tolerability studies, information on clinical 
outcomes requires further evaluation in a controlled and adequately powered study. Another 
limitation is generalizability: restricting eligibility to patients with a minimum number of convulsive 
seizures may limit applicability to those with fewer seizures. 
Real-world implementation of zorevunersen treatment may involve considerations such as 
sedation for intrathecal injections, interdisciplinary care needs, and ongoing monitoring to optimize 
patient outcomes.  
Overall, these data support the potential for disease modification with zorevunersen. 
Further assessment in a randomized, controlled Phase 3 trial is underway. These data also support 


===PAGE 19===
the selection of initial and continued doses of 70 mg and 45 mg zorevunersen, respectively, in the 
ongoing Phase 3 trial.
